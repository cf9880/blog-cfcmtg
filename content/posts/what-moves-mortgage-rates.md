**Mortgage rates are driven mostly by the bond market, not directly by the Federal Reserve. Investors buy mortgage-backed securities, and the yields they demand rise or fall with inflation, economic news, and expectations about the Fed. That's why mortgage rates can move even on days when the Fed does nothing.**

## Key Takeaways

- The **Fed sets short-term rates**, not mortgage rates.
- Mortgage rates follow **mortgage-backed securities (MBS)** and tend to move with the **10-year Treasury yield**.
- **Inflation data** is one of the biggest rate movers.
- Freddie Mac's 30-year average was **7.40%** the week of October 15, 2026, up from **6.27%** a year earlier.
- The Fed **raised rates on September 16, 2026**, and meets again **October 27-28**.

## Does the Federal Reserve Set Mortgage Rates?

No. The Fed sets the **federal funds rate**, the rate banks charge each other for overnight loans. That rate affects things like credit cards and home equity lines of credit more directly.

Mortgage rates are long-term rates. They're tied to what investors will pay for **mortgage-backed securities**, which are bundles of home loans sold to investors. When investors want a higher return on those bonds, mortgage rates go up.

The Fed still matters, though. Its decisions and comments shape what investors expect for inflation and the economy.

## What Did the Fed Do Recently?

On **September 16, 2026**, the Federal Reserve raised its target range by a quarter point to **3.75% to 4%**, in a 12-0 vote. Its statement said "inflation remains elevated" ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260916a.htm)).

Minutes from that meeting, released October 7, said most officials thought **another increase would likely be appropriate by year end**. The next meeting is **October 27-28, 2026**, per the [Fed's calendar](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm).

## Why Do Inflation Reports Move Mortgage Rates?

Bonds pay a fixed amount. When prices rise faster, those fixed payments are worth less, so investors demand higher yields to make up for it.

That's why monthly inflation reports matter so much:

- **CPI (Consumer Price Index):** Tracks prices consumers pay.
- **PPI (Producer Price Index):** Tracks prices businesses receive.
- **PCE:** The Fed's preferred inflation gauge.

Mid-October showed how messy this can get. Mortgage News Daily reported that core CPI came in hotter than expected on October 14 and core PPI rose 0.7% versus a 0.3% forecast on October 15. Yet bonds rallied anyway that week, helped by a "flight to safety" tied to worries about the tech sector ([Mortgage News Daily](https://www.mortgagenewsdaily.com/)). Rates don't always react the way headlines suggest.

## What Else Moves Mortgage Rates?

### The Economy and Jobs

Strong job growth and spending can push rates up because they can fuel inflation. Weak data often pushes rates down.

### Global Events and Market Fear

When investors get nervous, they often buy safe assets like U.S. Treasuries. That can lower yields and mortgage rates, at least for a while.

### Government Borrowing

When the government issues lots of debt, the extra supply of bonds can push yields higher.

### Your Own Profile

The market sets the baseline. Your own rate also depends on your **credit score, down payment, loan type, loan amount, property type, and points**. That's why two people can get different rates on the same day.

## Should I Lock My Rate or Wait?

A **rate lock** holds your rate for a set period, often 30 to 60 days, while your loan closes. **Floating** means waiting in hopes rates fall.

- **Lock** if your budget is tight or you're closing soon.
- **Float** only if you can handle a higher payment if rates rise.
