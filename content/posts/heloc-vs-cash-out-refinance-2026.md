**A HELOC is usually the better choice in late 2026 if your current mortgage rate is well below today's rates, because it leaves that first mortgage untouched. A cash-out refinance can make more sense if your rate is already near today's level, you want one fixed payment, or you need a large lump sum.**

With 30-year rates above 7% and many homeowners holding loans from earlier, cheaper years, the way you borrow against your equity matters as much as how much you borrow. Here's how the two options compare right now.

## Key Takeaways

- **Rates today:** Freddie Mac's 30-year average was 7.24% on October 29, 2026, and Bankrate's national average HELOC rate was 7.36% on October 28.
- **A cash-out refi replaces your whole mortgage** at today's rate. A HELOC adds a second loan and keeps your first one as is.
- **HELOCs are usually variable** and tied to the prime rate, which is 7.00% after the Fed's September 2026 hike.
- **Cash-out refis bring full closing costs** on the entire new balance. HELOC costs are often lower, but terms vary by lender.
- **Run the blended math** before you decide. The lower rate on paper isn't always the lower total cost.

## What Is the Difference Between a HELOC and a Cash-Out Refinance?

A **cash-out refinance** pays off your current mortgage with a new, larger one. You receive the difference in cash at closing, and your entire balance moves to the new rate and term.

A **home equity line of credit (HELOC)** is a separate, second loan secured by your home. It works like a credit line: you draw what you need during a draw period, often 10 years, then repay over a set repayment period. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-home-equity-line-of-credit-heloc-en-1001/) explains that most HELOCs carry variable rates, so payments can rise or fall.

## When Does a HELOC Make More Sense?

A HELOC often wins when:

- **Your first mortgage rate is low.** Keeping a loan at 3% to 5% untouched can save far more than any rate difference on the new money.
- **You need money in stages,** such as a remodel paid out over several months. You pay interest only on what you draw.
- **The amount is modest** compared with your total mortgage balance.
- **You plan to pay it down quickly,** for example with a bonus or the sale of another property.

The trade-off is rate risk. If prime rises, so does your HELOC payment. Some lenders let you lock part of the balance at a fixed rate, so ask.

## When Does a Cash-Out Refinance Make More Sense?

A cash-out refinance can be the better fit when:

- **Your current rate is already near today's rates,** so you give up little by refinancing.
- **You want one fixed, predictable payment** instead of two loans with one variable.
- **You need a large lump sum,** such as paying off high-interest debt all at once.
- **You want to change loan terms anyway,** like removing a co-borrower or switching from an ARM to a fixed rate.

Conventional cash-out refinances on a one-unit primary home are generally capped at 80% of the home's value. FHA and VA cash-out programs have their own limits.

## What Does the Math Look Like?

Here's a simple example. Say your home is worth $450,000, you owe $250,000 at 4.0%, and your principal and interest payment is $1,450. You want $60,000.

| Option | New monthly cost (principal and interest) |
|---|---|
| Cash-out refi to $310,000 at 7.27%, 30 years | About $2,119 total |
| Keep first mortgage + $60,000 HELOC at 7.36%, interest-only draw period | About $1,450 + $368 = $1,818 total |

In this example, the HELOC costs about $300 less per month, and you keep your 4.0% rate on the $250,000. HELOC payments rise once repayment begins, or if prime goes up, so compare the full timeline. Rates here are market averages for illustration, not a quote.

## What About Closing Costs and Taxes?

A cash-out refinance carries closing costs on the whole new loan, including title, appraisal, and lender fees. HELOC costs are often lower, but watch for annual fees, early-closure fees, and minimum draw rules.

On taxes, IRS [Publication 936](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p936) says interest on home equity debt is generally deductible only when the money is used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home that secures the loan. Talk with a tax professional about your situation.
